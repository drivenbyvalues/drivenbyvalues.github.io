# America's Single Bet vs. China's Many — The Case for a New GI Bill Moment

## The robot that started this

A few weeks ago I watched a Unitree humanoid dance, throw a backflip, and fold laundry in the same reel. It wasn't the choreography that stuck with me — it was the run. Unitree's newest model has been clocked at over 12.6 meters per second, faster than Usain Bolt's peak stride, and its jump height sits well past six feet. This isn't a lab curiosity: Unitree shipped more than 5,500 humanoid units in 2025 alone, and its August 2026 Shanghai IPO priced the company near a $9B valuation before shares surged 460–629% on debut day, pushing the implied market cap toward $66B.

I sat with that for a while, then asked the obvious question: where's the American equivalent, and why does it feel like we only have one horse in this race — and it's the same horse as everyone else's?

## One bet, many bets

Almost every dollar of venture and growth capital in the US right now is chasing the same narrow thesis: foundation models and the compute to train them. That's not irrational — it's a real, generational technology. But a portfolio with one position isn't a strategy, it's a bet. And while American capital has piled into that single trade, China has been running a diversified book across half a dozen frontier industries at once: humanoid robotics, solar and battery manufacturing, electric vehicles, rare earth processing, STEM education, and physical infrastructure. Some of those bets are propped up by state subsidies and will end in overcapacity and write-offs. Some are running on debt that will eventually come due. But directionally, diversification is out-executing concentration, and it's worth looking at the scoreboard domain by domain before deciding that doesn't matter.

| Domain | China's position | US position |
|---|---|---|
| Humanoid robotics | 5,500+ units shipped in 2025; MIIT targets 100,000 deployed by 2027 | Optimus missed its 2025 target; Boston Dynamics and Figure AI still pre-scale |
| Solar | ~300–370 GW installed in 2025 (more than the rest of the world combined); ~85–90% of global panel manufacturing | ~21 GW installed in 2025; residential solar credit eliminated in 2025 legislation |
| EVs | BYD (2.26M BEVs) passed Tesla (1.64M) globally in 2025; ~53–55% of new car sales are EVs | Tesla still leads domestically; EV penetration ~10% of new sales |
| Rare earths | ~70% of mining, ~90% of refining, ~94% of magnet production | ~13% of mining, one new refiner (MP Materials) scaling toward 2028 |
| Debt leverage | 3rd-largest foreign Treasury holder (~$650–760B, 2025–26) atop a record $735B current-account surplus | ~$39T gross debt; interest expense (~$1T) now exceeds defense spending |
| High-speed rail | 50,000 km in service, 75%+ of world total | ~735 km (Amtrak Acela only) |
| STEM graduates | ~2M STEM bachelor's/year; projected 77,000+ STEM PhDs/year | ~900,000 STEM bachelor's/year; ~40,000 STEM PhDs/year |

None of these numbers exist in isolation. They're the visible output of a government that decided, roughly a decade ago, to place simultaneous bets on the industries it expected to matter in 2030 — and then funded all of them at once instead of picking a winner and starving the rest.

## Robotics: the demo that isn't just a demo

Boston Dynamics — now SoftBank-owned — unveiled its Electric Atlas at CES 2026: 56 degrees of freedom, a 50 kg lift capacity, and pilot deployments at Hyundai and Google DeepMind. It's genuinely impressive engineering, and Korean brokerages have floated speculative pre-IPO valuations as high as $88–103B for it. Figure AI has partnerships with BMW and OpenAI, though its own valuation figures swing wildly across funding rounds ($2.6B to $39B) depending on which raise you're looking at — a sign of how thin the actual revenue base still is under the headline numbers.

The difference isn't the hardware — American engineering is still very much in the fight. The difference is deployment tempo and the capital stack underneath it. China's National AI Industry Investment Fund has put $8.2B behind the sector since January 2025; Shenzhen alone committed roughly $1.4B in a single city fund weeks later. TrendForce now expects global humanoid shipments to cross 50,000 units in 2026, up sevenfold year over year — and Unitree's price point (a fraction of Optimus's projected $20–30K) suggests China intends to win humanoid robotics the same way it won solar panels and EV batteries: not by having the single best robot, but by making millions of good-enough ones cheaper than anyone else can.

## Energy: built at a scale policy can't undo

In the first half of 2025, China installed 256 GW of solar — more than the entire rest of the world combined that period. The US, by comparison, installed about 21 GW for the full year. That gap isn't just about ambition; it's structural. China makes roughly 85–90% of the world's solar panels, wafers, and cells, which means it controls the supply chain regardless of who's buying. Meanwhile, the "One Big Beautiful Bill" signed in July 2025 eliminated the residential solar tax credit outright at the end of 2025 — not a phase-down, a cliff — after fifteen years of the opposite policy signal under the IRA.

That whiplash is the real cost. It's not that America can't build renewable capacity; it's that American energy policy resets every four to eight years depending on who wins an election, while Chinese industrial policy compounds for a decade at a time. Compounding beats resetting, every time, regardless of which system is "better" on paper.

## EVs: BYD's 2025 overtake

For the first time, a Chinese automaker outsold Tesla globally in battery-electric vehicles: BYD delivered roughly 2.26M BEVs in 2025 against Tesla's 1.64M, growing 28% while Tesla's global deliveries fell. It's worth being precise here — Tesla still dominates the US market itself, and BYD has essentially zero US passenger-vehicle presence. This isn't a story about American EVs losing at home. It's a story about who's winning everywhere else: China exported 8.32M vehicles in 2025, with EV and hybrid exports up 70% to 3.43M units, reaching roughly 90 countries. Geely's exports alone grew over 100% year over year. While Tesla and Lucid compete for the US and parts of Europe, BYD and Geely are building the default car brand for the rest of the world.

## Rare earths: the chokehold nobody planned for

This is the domain where diversification failure turns into direct leverage. China controls about 70% of rare earth mining but, more importantly, about 90% of refining and 94% of magnet production — the parts of the supply chain that are actually hard to replicate. In April and again in October 2025, China expanded export controls on medium and heavy rare earths, adding an extraterritorial rule requiring licenses for any foreign product containing even trace Chinese-origin rare earth content. The Pentagon responded in July 2025 by taking a $400M stake in MP Materials, becoming its largest shareholder and guaranteeing above-market magnet prices for a decade — a second facility won't come online until roughly 2028. That's the shape of the problem: it took a single-source supply shock to produce the first serious US industrial policy response in this sector, three years after the warning signs were obvious.

## Education: the compounding asset nobody's compounding

Underneath every other domain is the one this article is nominally filed under: human capital. China produces roughly 2M STEM bachelor's degrees a year against the US's 900,000, and is projected to graduate nearly double the STEM PhDs. Meanwhile, 2025 brought over 5,300 frozen or terminated NIH and NSF grants in the US, totaling more than $5B, and a proposed FY2026 budget that would have cut NSF by 57% and NIH by over 40% before Congress partially restored funding. K-12 fared similarly — an early 2025 proposal to cut federal K-12 grant funding by roughly 70% was walked back to flat funding by February 2026, but "flat" after years of inflation is still a real-terms cut. You don't out-innovate a country that's doubling its STEM graduate pipeline by making your own funding unpredictable year to year.

## Debt: the leverage nobody's supposed to talk about

Underneath all of this sits a number that makes every other domain harder to fund: US gross national debt is roughly $39 trillion, about 80% of it held by the public and financed largely by whoever is willing to keep buying Treasuries. Foreign holders own about $9.1 trillion of that — Japan is the largest single holder at roughly $1.15 trillion, the UK second, and China third at somewhere between $650 and $760 billion depending on which month's Treasury data you read (China's true exposure is murkier still, since some of it is held indirectly through UK and Belgian custodial accounts). China's holdings have fallen by 40–50% from their 2013 peak, even as its current-account surplus hit a record $735 billion in 2025 — the money isn't gone, it's being redirected into gold and other non-dollar assets instead of recycled back into US debt the way it used to be.

The popular version of the risk here is that China could "dump" its Treasuries and crash the US economy. The mechanism behind the first half of that is real and not speculative: a large, fast sale would push Treasury yields up, raising US borrowing costs, and 2025 already gave a preview — a weak 20-year bond auction in the spring, with foreign (indirect) bidders taking only two-thirds of the offering, coincided with the 10-year yield jumping from about 4.0% to over 4.4% amid tariff-driven uncertainty. Interest on the debt is no longer a rounding error: at roughly $1 trillion a year, it now exceeds US defense spending, which means every basis point foreign demand pushes yields up is a direct hit to the federal budget, competing with the same industrial and education investments this article is arguing for.

The second half of the popular story — a full "dump" as a deliberate weapon — is weaker than it sounds, and it's worth being precise about why. Selling $700 billion of anything quickly depresses the price of what you're selling before you finish selling it, and converting those dollars back to yuan would appreciate China's own currency, undercutting the export competitiveness its whole growth model depends on. It's the financial equivalent of a hand grenade thrown across a table with no wall behind you either. Brad Setser, a former Treasury economist now at the Council on Foreign Relations, has made the more interesting point explicitly: China's real leverage today isn't the Treasury pile at all — it's rare earths, critical minerals, and magnets, the physical inputs "the Fed can't print." The debt overhang is a genuine vulnerability, but it's a slow-burning one, a tax on every other bet the country needs to place, not the loaded gun it gets described as. The faster-acting chokehold is the one covered above.

## Infrastructure: built vs. promised

China's high-speed rail network reached 50,000 km in 2025 — over three-quarters of the world's total, carrying 4.28 billion passenger trips a year. The US has about 735 km of anything resembling high-speed rail, all of it the Acela corridor. California's High-Speed Rail project is the starkest case study: budgeted at $33B in 2008, now estimated near $126B, with no track yet in revenue service and the FRA pulling $4B in federal grants in July 2025 over missed procurement deadlines. Infrastructure spending as a share of GDP tells the same story at the macro level: roughly 5.5% a year in China versus about 1.5% in the US. Neither number is inherently right — China's overbuilding has produced real waste and debt — but the US number reflects decades of chronic underinvestment, not disciplined restraint.

## The honest caveats

None of this is an argument that China's model is one to copy wholesale. State-directed capital produces overcapacity — solar and EV manufacturing in China are already showing signs of it, with price wars eating margins across the industry. Export controls cut both ways and invite retaliation. And the same government funding this diversification also runs an authoritarian system with no interest in importing alongside its industrial policy. The point isn't "become China." The point is narrower and more uncomfortable: a country that places six serious bets will beat a country that places one, even if several of the six turn out badly, because optionality compounds and concentration doesn't.

## The 1944 playbook

The US has done this before. The original GI Bill sent roughly 2 million veterans to college on the government's dime; by 1947, veterans made up 49% of all US college enrollment. It funded VA-guaranteed home loans that built the postwar suburbs, unemployment support, and job training — and economists broadly credit it with manufacturing the mid-century American middle class more or less on purpose. It's also worth saying plainly that the bill's benefits were administered unevenly: Black veterans and women were frequently excluded in practice from housing and education benefits by discriminatory local administration. A 2026 version has to fix that flaw, not just scale up the original.

## What a 2026 version could look like

Not a single new program, but the same structural idea applied across the domains above: federal capital that compounds for a decade regardless of election cycles, aimed at more than one industry at once. Debt-free technical and STEM education tied to national capacity needs — robotics, battery chemistry, grid engineering, rare earth processing — the way the original GI Bill was tied to reabsorbing a wartime economy into a peacetime one. Infrastructure funding structured so a change in administration can't zero out a project already under construction. A rare earth and critical minerals buildout that doesn't wait for the next export-control shock to get funded. None of this requires becoming a state-planned economy. It requires deciding, the way the 1944 Congress did and the way Beijing has for the last decade, that broad-based public investment is what makes the next S-curve of growth land on the middle class instead of past it.

## Closing

I don't think America is out of the race in any of these domains. Boston Dynamics' engineering is real; MP Materials' backing is real; the US still has the deepest capital markets and research universities on earth. But a country that's watching one Chinese company's IPO pop 500% in a single day, on a robot that runs faster than an Olympic sprinter, should be asking why its own capital is stacked six-deep in a single trade instead of spread across the six or seven industries that will actually determine what the next decade of American jobs, wages, and geopolitical leverage look like. The 1950s reboot isn't nostalgia. It's just what diversification looks like at a national scale — and it's the only version of this story where "catching up" and "the middle class prospers again" are the same sentence.
